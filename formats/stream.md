# Stream and video clip format guide

Use this guide for every batch, for any streamer or YouTuber, any game or category: gameplay, Just Chatting, IRL, podcasts, regular YouTube videos. These are starting editorial settings. If a preset exists for the source's game or category (`formats/<preset>.md`, e.g. `gta6.md`), apply it on top of this guide; where they differ, the preset wins. If you change a setting, give the revised guide to the full workflow for that batch.

## What makes a moment worth clipping

Before judging the recording yourself, check what other people already clipped from the same stream, video or creator (Twitch clips of the VOD, YouTube Shorts and clip channels). A moment that someone else clipped and that earned views is proven: give it priority, as long as it also works under this guide's length and context rules. Popular clip titles for the creator show which kinds of moment their audience shares.

Scout the whole recording for anything worth replaying. Give priority to high-energy moments wherever they happen. When the source has a facecam, watch the main picture (gameplay, screen share, the scene) and the facecam independently throughout: either can carry a clip on its own, and sometimes the timing between them makes the moment. Flag what catches your attention even during downtime. For each candidate, identify the frame or line that makes it worth watching and the setup a new viewer needs. Keep scouting after the first strong highlight.

Preserve the same visual format across clips and tailor the edit to each payoff. Give viewers a beat to spot a background detail, hold suspense through a near miss, or end a joke on the reaction. When two clips score similarly, lead with a different kind of moment. A single event can yield two clips (for example a stunt and a later joke) if each stands on its own.

## Picture

Create every edited preview and final clip on a 9:16 portrait canvas at 720p (720 pixels wide × 1280 pixels high). Apply this to main-picture-only, main picture with facecam, and facecam-only edits. Verify the exported dimensions and square pixels before delivery. Export an MP4 with H.264 video, AAC audio and the source frame rate up to 60 fps. Stack the hook title at the top, then the facecam panel (when there is one), then the captions, then the main picture. The main picture fills the bottom of the canvas edge to edge, cropped in from the sides just enough to fit the 9:16 frame, never showing the stream's own overlays or HUD widgets (counters, alerts, chat boxes). Behind the hook, facecam and captions, fill the canvas with a synchronized duplicate, enlarged, blurred and darkened. Keep the action inside the crop; when it spans the full width (two things approaching from opposite edges), show the entire wide shot instead. Zoom further only when the viewer can still see what causes the payoff.

Use Higgsfield Seedance 2.5 for all generated video elements. Select the exact model and request 9:16 at 720p. If the model is unavailable, mark generation pending. Preserve original footage and audio.

## Facecam and main picture

When a moment includes facecam, show both in the vertical edit. Start with the camera above the main picture. Keep an embedded overlay in place if it is already readable and leaves the action visible. Play the original audio once and keep face, speech and action in sync. Facecam overlays differ between creators and even between streams of the same creator: measure the facecam and the clean main-picture area on a frame from each batch.

Scout the camera throughout, including menus, loading screens and quiet stretches. Flag funny lines, interesting observations, expressions and unexpected activity in the room. If the moment makes sense with the main picture hidden, make a separate facecam-only candidate. Use the clean camera track when available, or crop the original overlay at a readable size. Keep enough of the room in frame to see what happened and keep subtitles clear of it. Use the camera image for any background fill. A full-screen camera may show a guest or another creator: check who it is before calling it a facecam clip.

Rank camera-only clips alongside the other edits. Two clips can share a timestamp when each delivers a distinct payoff, such as an on-screen stunt and a pet knocking something over behind the streamer. If a reaction needs the main picture to explain it, keep the combined edit. Sources without a facecam (most YouTube videos, IRL) use the main picture alone.

## Timing and hook

Keep every clip short enough to hold attention start to finish: aim for 10 to 25 seconds, and never exceed 30 seconds. Open on the first line or action that makes a new viewer curious, ideally within a second or two of the setup. Keep only the setup needed to understand the turn; a pause, glance or failed attempt stays only when it builds anticipation. Give the punchline and the creator's genuine reaction room to land, then end as soon as the last funny beat registers. If an escalation can't fit in 30 seconds, keep only its best beat.

Every clip must make sense to someone who has never seen the creator. Skip moments that depend on earlier context: inside jokes, callbacks, ongoing storylines, giveaway or chat admin, sponsor reads. Use a moment that needs context only when that context is visible on screen or said within the first few seconds of the clip.

Keep the facecam prominent when the expression carries the joke. Chat can flag the moment. Time subtitles to the actual delivery so the punchline first appears when it is spoken. Keep any opening text short and spoiler-free.

## Captions and sound

Use Barlow Bold in sentence case. On a 720 × 1280 canvas, start at 50 px for subtitles and 58 px for hooks, with white lettering, a 3 px charcoal outline and a subtle shadow. Use warm yellow on one word when emphasis helps a line land. Set two lines maximum, break at natural phrases, and preview at phone size. Keep captions steady through delivery. Keep text inside the middle 80% of the frame width and above the bottom 20%. Move it when it covers a face, an on-screen marker or the action. Transcribe the actual speech, verify names by ear and time each phrase to its speaker. Preserve a silent visual gag. Prioritize dialogue and meaningful sounds. Add a quiet licensed accent only when it draws attention to something real. Omit added music when the original reaction carries the scene.

## What keeps a viewer watching

The Scout identifies the reason to stay after the first frame and the exact moment the viewer gets the payoff. The Editor opens on that question or action, cuts repeated information, and keeps any pause that builds anticipation. The Producer watches once as a newcomer and marks the first point where interest slips. Test a shorter setup or clearer opening when needed, then check that the reaction still lands. Use actual audience retention when available to evaluate these judgments.

## Selection and delivery

Deliver exactly 10 clips per batch, ranked from best to worst. Rank by how quickly and clearly the payoff lands for a stranger, the strength of the first two seconds, proven demand from clips others already posted, and rewatch or share value; at equal quality the shorter clip ranks higher. Shortlist more than 10 so that 10 survive review. Keep distinct moments a new viewer can understand. One event earns one posting slot unless a second cut tells a genuinely different story.

Split the 10 into two groups, keeping the overall rank:

- **Proven:** the moment overlaps one that other people already posted and that blew up for this creator (top 5% of the source's posted moments by views, at least 50 views). Lower risk; the evidence (views, titles) goes with the post.
- **Fresh:** nobody has posted the moment yet, or only to a handful of views. Your own find: the chance to be first.

There is no quota for either group; rank purely on quality. Deliver a playable MP4 per clip with its post ready to paste (see Posting), a short title, source credit, a source timestamp and one sentence explaining the choice. Hold a clip with unresolved publishing permission or unclear third-party audio for review; held clips don't count toward the 10. Keep drafts separate from the posting queue.

## Posting

Every clip leaves post-ready. For each one, write:

- **Title:** short and spoiler-free, the same promise as the hook (used as the YouTube Shorts title, with #shorts).
- **Caption:** one line, 150 characters at most, in a casual viewer's voice: the relatable reaction or question, not a summary. One emoji at most. No spoilers of the payoff.
- **Source credit:** the creator and platform on every post (for example "🎥 CreatorName on Twitch"), and the source link in YouTube descriptions.
- **Hashtags:** 4 to 8, lowercase, no spaces. Mix 2 to 3 for the moment and a relatable situation (#baddriver, #roommates, #whenyourfriend), 1 to 2 from tags that real clips of this creator or source already use, and the evergreen tags below. Every tag must fit the clip; never add a popular tag that doesn't (another game, unrelated trends). Skip filler like #fyp and #viral.
- **Evergreen tags:** the game or category tag (a preset may name it), the creator's name tag, and #twitchclips for Twitch sources.

Platform blocks are produced for TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts and X from the same title, caption, credit and hashtags.

## Example edit

Illustrative footage: a driver says “I know a shortcut,” turns down an alley, and meets the same police cruiser at the other end. Start on “shortcut.” Keep both the alley entrance and the cruiser visible. Caption the line, let the sound of the brakes carry the reveal, and end after the driver’s reaction. A fitting title is “He knew a shortcut.” A spike of laughter in chat is a cue to rewind and find the turn.

## Run a batch

Paste: “Clip [link]. Make 10 clips, ranked best to worst, and return the MP4s with their posts.” Any Twitch VOD or YouTube video works, from any creator. Attach an updated copy of this guide to override the bundled settings. For a broadcast in progress, live capture and timestamped chat must be available to the session or supplied by a recorder. If available, include a timestamped chat export with the recording.

The cross-check against clips others posted runs on its own once the environment has: a Twitch developer app (`TWITCH_CLIENT_ID` and `TWITCH_CLIENT_SECRET`) for Twitch clips, and for YouTube a YouTube Data API key (searches and view counts, never expires) plus cookies from a spare Google account (`YT_COOKIES_B64`, needed only to match YouTube clips' audio; refresh every few weeks).
